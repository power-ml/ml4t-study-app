G1: Portfolio Weights and Constraints 
[QF-D4G1Q1] 
A university endowment allocates 40% to equities, 35% to bonds, 15% to real estate, and 10% to cash. Its policy requires 
the portfolio to be fully invested and prohibits both borrowing and short selling. 
 
A. The stated weights sum to 100%, so the portfolio is fully invested. 
B. Under a long-only rule, every asset weight must be greater than or equal to zero. 
 
 318 Rev:081020261230 
C. A weight of -10% in bonds would comply with the policy as long as the other weights were adjusted to keep the sum at 
100%. 
D. If the weights summed to 90%, the remaining 10% could be interpreted as unallocated cash only if cash were not 
already represented as an explicit asset weight. 
E. The sum-of-weights requirement alone prevents the optimizer from choosing negative weights. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – The four weights add to 1.00, satisfying the fully invested requirement. 
B. True – Long-only constraints rule out negative positions. 
C. False – A negative bond weight is a short position and violates the stated policy. 
D. True – Whether a residual represents cash depends on how the model deﬁnes its investable assets and budget 
constraint. 
E. False – Weights can sum to one while some are negative and others exceed one unless separate constraints prohibit 
that behavior. 
 
[QF-D4G1Q2] 
A hedge fund proposes weights of 120% in Stock A, 30% in Stock B, and -50% in Stock C. The mandate permits short 
selling and requires net exposure of 100%, but the risk committee is reviewing the allocation's leverage. 
 
A. The weights sum to 100%, so the portfolio satisﬁes the stated net-exposure requirement. 
B. The -50% weight represents a short position in Stock C. 
C. Because the weights sum to 100%, the portfolio has no leverage. 
D. The portfolio's gross exposure is not 200%, calculated as the sum of the absolute weights. 
E. Imposing a long-only constraint would not make this exact allocation infeasible. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – The net weights are 1.20 + 0.30 - 0.50 = 1.00. 
B. True – A negative asset weight represents a short exposure. 
C. False – Net exposure can equal 100% even when offsetting long and short positions create leverage. 
D. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Gross exposure is |1.20| + |0.30| + |-0.50| = 2.00, or 200%. 
E. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. The negative weight violates a long-only requirement.

A retirement plan optimizes among ﬁve funds subject to a 5% minimum and a 35% maximum weight in each fund. It must 
remain fully invested, and the investment committee is checking whether several proposed allocations are feasible. 
 
A. Assigning 35% to each of three funds would not violate the fully invested constraint before allocating anything to the 
other two funds. 
B. Assigning exactly 5% to a fund satisﬁes that fund's minimum-weight constraint. 
C. A proposed 40% weight in one fund is feasible because the other four weights could be reduced enough to preserve a 
100% total. 
D. The ﬁve 5% minimums require at least 25% of the portfolio to be allocated before the optimizer distributes the 
remaining 75%. 
E. Position bounds can change which portfolio the optimizer selects even when expected returns and the covariance

Answer Key: 
 
A. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Three 35% positions already total 105%, leaving no feasible

B. True – A bound is satisﬁed at its endpoint unless the constraint is explicitly strict. 
C. False – A 40% position exceeds the stated 35% maximum regardless of the other weights. 
D. True – Five required allocations of 5% account for 25% of total capital. 
E. True – Constraints change the feasible set and can therefore change the optimum. 
 
G2: Minimum-Variance Portfolios 
[QF-D4G2Q1] 
A risk-averse foundation asks for the global minimum-variance portfolio among its approved assets. One trustee 
assumes this must be the portfolio holding only the asset with the lowest individual volatility. 
 
A. The global minimum-variance portfolio is the feasible portfolio with the lowest portfolio variance. 
B. It cannot combine multiple assets if their imperfect co-movement reduces risk below that of the least-volatile asset 
alone. 
C. Identifying it requires covariance information, not merely a ranking of individual asset volatilities. 
D. It is deﬁned as the feasible portfolio with the highest expected return. 
E. Its expected return cannot be lower than that of another feasible portfolio. 
 
 320 Rev:081020261230 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – The global minimum-variance portfolio minimizes variance over the entire feasible set. 
B. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Diversiﬁcation can make a combination less volatile than any 
component considered independently. 
C. True – Portfolio variance depends on both stand-alone variances and cross-asset covariances. 
D. False – Minimum variance and maximum expected return are different objectives. 
E. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Minimizing risk does not require maximizing expected return. 
 
[QF-D4G2Q2] 
An optimizer considers two assets with equal 20% annual volatility and a correlation of -0.50. The manager compares 
investing entirely in either asset with a 50/50 allocation. 
 
A. The variance of either single-asset portfolio is 0.0400. 
B. The covariance between the two assets is -0.0200. 
C. The 50/50 portfolio variance is not 0.0100. 
D. The 50/50 portfolio volatility is 10%. 
E. If the correlation rose to +1 while all other inputs stayed ﬁxed, the 50/50 portfolio would retain the same 10% volatility. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – Variance is 0.20² = 0.0400. 
B. True – Covariance is -0.50 × 0.20 × 0.20 = -0.0200. 
C. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Portfolio variance is 0.25(0.0400) + 0.25(0.0400) + 
2(0.50)(0.50)(-0.0200) = 0.0100. 
D. True – The square root of 0.0100 is 0.10, or 10%. 
E. False – At perfect positive correlation, equal-volatility assets provide no volatility reduction and the portfolio volatility 
would be 20%.

requirement, the optimizer chooses a portfolio with a 6% expected return and lower volatility.

solved correctly. 
B. The new portfolio may be the global minimum-variance portfolio because no target return above 6% is imposed. 
C. Removing a constraint cannot enlarge the feasible set. 
D. The optimizer's decision proves that 6% is the maximum achievable expected return. 
E. A portfolio with a higher expected return cannot also have higher variance than the global minimum-variance portfolio.

B. True – Without a target-return restriction, the variance objective can select the lowest-risk feasible portfolio overall. 
C. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Every formerly feasible solution remains available, and 
additional lower-return portfolios may become eligible. 
D. False – The objective is risk minimization, so its selected return does not establish the maximum feasible return. 
E. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Moving away from the global minimum-variance point can trade 
greater risk for greater expected return. 
 
G3: Maximum-Sharpe Portfolios 
[QF-D4G3Q1] 
With a 2% risk-free rate, an optimizer compares Portfolio A, with a 10% expected return and 16% volatility, against 
Portfolio B, with an 8% expected return and 8% volatility. 
 
A. Portfolio A's expected excess return is 8%. 
B. Portfolio A's Sharpe ratio is not 0.50. 
C. Portfolio B's Sharpe ratio is 0.75. 
D. Portfolio A has the higher expected return and therefore must be the maximum-Sharpe choice. 
E. Based only on these two candidates and the stated inputs, Portfolio B has the higher expected excess return per unit 
of volatility. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – Expected excess return is 10% - 2% = 8%.

B. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Portfolio A's Sharpe ratio is 0.08 / 0.16 = 0.50. 
C. True – Portfolio B's Sharpe ratio is (0.08 - 0.02) / 0.08 = 0.75. 
D. False – Maximizing Sharpe considers both excess return and volatility, not expected return alone. 
E. True – Portfolio B's Sharpe ratio exceeds Portfolio A's under the stated assumptions. 
 
[QF-D4G3Q2] 
A mean-variance analyst identiﬁes a tangency portfolio of risky assets and explains its role to an investment committee 
that can also lend or borrow at the modeled risk-free rate. 
 
A. The tangency portfolio is the risky portfolio with the maximum Sharpe ratio under the model's inputs and feasible set. 
B. Graphically, the capital allocation line through the risk-free asset is tangent to the risky-asset efficient frontier at that 
portfolio. 
C. Every investor must hold only the tangency portfolio and no risk-free asset, regardless of risk tolerance. 
D. Investors cannot combine the tangency portfolio with the risk-free asset to select different overall risk levels under the 
standard assumptions. 
E. The tangency portfolio is necessarily the same as the global minimum-variance risky portfolio. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – It maximizes modeled excess return per unit of volatility among feasible risky portfolios. 
B. True – The line with the greatest slope from the risk-free intercept touches the frontier at the tangency portfolio. 
C. False – Risk tolerance affects the mix between the tangency portfolio and risk-free borrowing or lending. 
D. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Changing that mix moves the investor along the capital 
allocation line. 
E. False – The minimum-variance and maximum-Sharpe objectives generally select different portfolios.

B. At a 1% risk-free rate, Portfolio Y's Sharpe ratio is 0.60. 
C. At a 5% risk-free rate, the two portfolios have equal Sharpe ratios of 0.40.

volatilities. 
E. Raising the risk-free rate increases both portfolios' expected excess returns. 
 
Answer Key:

G4: Efficient Frontier 
[QF-D4G4Q1] 
An investment committee compares three feasible portfolios. Portfolio A offers 7% expected return with 9% volatility, 
Portfolio B offers 7% with 12% volatility, and Portfolio C offers 9% with 12% volatility. 
 
A. Portfolio B is not dominated by Portfolio A because A offers the same expected return with less risk. 
B. Portfolio B is not also dominated by Portfolio C because C offers a higher expected return with the same risk. 
C. Portfolio B belongs on the efficient frontier because it lies between A and C numerically. 
D. An efficient portfolio cannot be improved by increasing expected return without increasing risk or by decreasing risk 
without decreasing expected return. 
E. Based only on these three choices, both A and C can be efficient. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. A provides an equal return at lower volatility. 
B. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. C provides a higher return at equal volatility. 
C. False – Numerical location alone does not overcome B's domination by other feasible choices. 
D. True – This expresses the risk-return tradeoff that deﬁnes mean-variance efficiency. 
E. True – Neither A nor C dominates the other because C's higher return comes with higher risk.

An optimizer traces the minimum achievable volatility for target expected returns of 5%, 7%, 9%, and 11%. The global 
minimum-variance portfolio has an expected return of 6%. 
 
A. For each target return, the optimizer seeks the lowest-risk feasible portfolio meeting the speciﬁed target. 
B. The upper branch beginning at the global minimum-variance portfolio forms the mean-variance efficient frontier. 
C. A minimum-variance portfolio targeting 5% return must be efficient even though the global minimum-variance 
portfolio offers 6% at no greater risk. 
D. Moving upward along the efficient frontier generally involves accepting more risk for a higher expected return. 
E. Every feasible portfolio lies on the efficient frontier. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – The frontier can be constructed by minimizing variance at successive expected-return targets. 
B. True – Portfolios above the global minimum-variance point provide the efficient risk-return tradeoffs. 
C. False – A portfolio below the global minimum-variance portfolio's return is dominated when the global minimum-
variance portfolio has at least as little risk and a higher return. 
D. True – Higher frontier returns ordinarily require higher volatility. 
E. False – Many feasible portfolios are interior or dominated and therefore inefficient.

G5: Optimization Inputs and Estimation Error 
[QF-D4G5Q1] 
A quant team runs a mean-variance optimizer using historical expected returns and covariances. The output assigns 78% 
of the portfolio to one small-cap stock and small short positions to several others. The chief investment officer asks 
whether the optimizer has uncovered a uniquely attractive opportunity. 
 
A. Small changes in expected-return estimates can produce large changes in optimized weights. 
B. An extreme allocation can reﬂect estimation error rather than genuine economic attractiveness. 
C. The optimizer’s output is guaranteed to remain optimal out of sample. 
D. Adding realistic position limits cannot produce a more stable portfolio. 
E. Historical optimization can overﬁt the sample used to estimate inputs. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – Mean-variance optimization can be highly sensitive to estimated inputs. 
B. True – Extreme weights may be artifacts of noisy forecasts. 
C. False – In-sample optimality does not guarantee future optimality. 
D. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Constraints can reduce instability and improve 
implementability. 
E. True – The optimizer may exploit sample-speciﬁc noise.

G6: Constraints and Practical Portfolio Design 
[QF-D4G6Q1] 
An unconstrained optimizer recommends investing 160% in equities, shorting 60% in bonds, and rebalancing most of the 
portfolio each month. A pension plan cannot short, permits no leverage, and wants to limit trading costs. 
 
A. The unconstrained allocation is not infeasible under the pension plan's long-only policy. 
B. A gross-exposure or leverage constraint can prevent the 160% long position ﬁnanced by the bond short. 
C. A turnover constraint cannot limit how far new weights move from current weights. 
D. Adding these constraints must improve the portfolio's modeled maximum Sharpe ratio. 
E. A constrained portfolio may sacriﬁce some in-sample objective value in exchange for implementability. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. The negative bond position violates the prohibition on short 
selling. 
B. True – Such a constraint limits the total amount of long and short exposure the optimizer can employ. 
C. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Turnover measures trading needed to transition between 
existing and proposed allocations. 
D. False – Restricting the feasible set cannot improve the maximum value of the same mathematical objective, though it 
may improve real-world robustness. 
E. True – Practical controls can trade an idealized model optimum for a portfolio the institution can hold and trade.

G7: In-Sample Optimization and Out-of-Sample Performance 
[QF-D4G7Q1] 
A researcher uses ten years of monthly returns to estimate inputs, choose portfolio weights, and report performance over 
those same ten years. The optimized portfolio substantially outperforms a simple equal-weight benchmark in that 
sample. 
 
A. The reported comparison is not in sample because the same observations inﬂuenced both the weights and the 
evaluation. 
B. Selecting the best weights for the historical sample can capitalize on noise that will not repeat. 
C. The in-sample outperformance guarantees superior future performance if the optimizer solved the problem correctly. 
D. Evaluating the ﬁxed weights on later, previously unused data would provide an out-of-sample test. 
E. Repeatedly revising the strategy after inspecting the test period can contaminate its status as an independent test. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. The data served both model selection and performance 
measurement. 
B. True – Optimization can ﬁt sample-speciﬁc return and covariance patterns. 
C. False – Solving the historical problem exactly does not make the estimated relationships stable in the future. 
D. True – Unused subsequent observations can test how the selected portfolio generalizes. 
E. True – Using test results to redesign the strategy turns that information into part of the development process.
