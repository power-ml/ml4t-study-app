G1: Price vs. Value 
[QF-D6G1Q1] 
A research analyst estimates that a stock is intrinsically worth $70 per share, while it currently trades at $55. The portfolio 
manager asks whether the price difference is sufficient evidence to buy immediately. 
 
A. The $55 market price is directly observable from current transactions or quotations. 
B. The $70 intrinsic value is an estimate that depends on the analyst's assumptions and model. 
C. If the analyst's estimate is correct, the stock appears undervalued by $15 per share. 
D. The difference between estimated value and market price guarantees a proﬁtable trade. 
E. Another informed analyst could not reasonably estimate a different intrinsic value. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – Market price is an observed trading value, unlike a model-derived estimate. 
B. True – Intrinsic value depends on inputs such as expected cash ﬂows, growth, and discount rates. 
C. True – The estimated value exceeds the market price by $70 - $55 = $15. 
D. False – The estimate may be wrong, and even correct value can fail to converge within the investor's horizon. 
E. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Reasonable analysts may use different forecasts, risk 
assessments, and valuation methods. 
 
[QF-D6G1Q2] 
Two analysts evaluate the same software company. Analyst A estimates a value of $40 using conservative growth and a 
12% discount rate. Analyst B estimates $65 using stronger growth and a 9% discount rate. The shares trade at $50, and 
the investment committee asks which analyst has identiﬁed the "true" value. 
 
A. The same market price can appear overvalued under one defensible model and undervalued under another. 
B. Analyst B's lower discount rate tends to increase estimated present value, all else equal. 
C. Analyst A's more conservative growth assumption tends to increase estimated value. 
D. The disagreement cannot arise from uncertainty rather than an arithmetic error by either analyst. 
E. The $50 market price proves that both intrinsic-value estimates are incorrect. 
 
Answer Key:

A. True – $50 exceeds Analyst A's estimate but is below Analyst B's estimate. 
B. True – A lower discount rate reduces the amount by which future cash ﬂows are discounted. 
C. False – Lower assumed growth generally reduces projected cash ﬂows and estimated value. 
D. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Valuation requires uncertain forecasts and judgments on which 
informed analysts may disagree. 
E. False – Market price records where trading occurs; it does not reveal a uniquely correct intrinsic value. 
 
[QF-D6G1Q3] 
A merger rumor pushes a company's shares from $30 to $44. A fundamental analyst's stand-alone valuation remains 
$34, while a completed acquisition could plausibly be worth $48. The fund must decide whether to trade before the rumor 
is conﬁrmed. 
 
A. The new $44 price can reﬂect the market's probability-weighted assessment of multiple outcomes. 
B. Comparing $44 only with the $34 stand-alone estimate is sufficient to prove that the stock is mispriced. 
C. If the acquisition fails, the price could fall even though the $44 price was reasonable given information available earlier. 
D. A valuation-based trade should consider both payoff magnitude and the probability of each scenario. 
E. Observing the price increase reveals with certainty that informed investors value the completed acquisition at exactly 
$44. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – Prices can incorporate possible deal and no-deal outcomes rather than a single certain outcome. 
B. False – The acquisition scenario and its probability are relevant to value while the rumor remains credible. 
C. True – A reasonable ex ante price can lead to a loss when an uncertain adverse outcome occurs. 
D. True – Expected value depends on both scenario payoffs and their probabilities. 
E. False – The price does not uniquely identify investors' scenario valuations or probability estimates.

[QF-D6G2Q1] 
A manufacturer reports $500 million in assets, $320 million in liabilities, and 30 million shares outstanding. Its shares 
trade at $9.00. The portfolio manager asks for a quick book-value assessment. 
 
A. Reported shareholder equity is not $180 million.

B. Reported book value per share is not $6.00. 
C. The stock's price-to-book ratio is 1.5. 
D. Trading above book value proves that the stock is overvalued. 
E. A later $60 million asset write-down, with liabilities unchanged, would reduce book value per share to $4.00. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Shareholder equity is assets minus liabilities: $500 million -

B. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. $180 million divided by 30 million shares equals $6.00 per

C. True – Price-to-book is $9.00 / $6.00 = 1.5. 
D. False – Market value may rationally exceed book value because of earning power, growth, or unrecorded intangible 
assets. 
E. True – Equity would fall to $120 million, or $4.00 for each of 30 million shares.

A. Internally developed human capital and client relationships may be incompletely reﬂected on the balance sheet. 
B. Book value is not based primarily on accounting measurements rather than a direct estimate of future earning power. 
C. A high price-to-book ratio is conclusive evidence of mispricing. 
D. Comparing price-to-book ratios is generally more informative when ﬁrms have similar business models and

E. If important productive assets are absent from reported book value, price-to-book can look high without the stock 
necessarily being overvalued.

A. True – Many valuable internally developed intangible resources are not recognized as balance-sheet assets. 
B. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Accounting book value measures recorded assets less 
liabilities, not the present value of all future proﬁts. 
C. False – The ratio must be interpreted in light of proﬁtability, asset composition, growth, and accounting choices.

E. True – A small accounting denominator can produce a high ratio even when market value is economically reasonable.

A. Reported book value cannot overstate the amount available to shareholders in liquidation.

cushion. 
D. True – Common equity is a residual claim after creditor obligations are satisﬁed. 
E. False – Asset markdowns, senior claims, and liquidation costs can leave nothing for common shareholders. 
 
G3: Time Value of Money 
[QF-D6G3Q1] 
A pension fund can receive $100 million today or a guaranteed $121 million two years from now. Its opportunity cost is 
10% annually, compounded annually. The investment committee asks whether the two choices are economically 
equivalent. 
 
A. The present value of $121 million received in two years is $100 million at a 10% discount rate. 
B. The future value of $100 million invested for two years at 10% is $121 million. 
 
 352 Rev:081020261230 
C. At the stated discount rate, the fund should strictly prefer the delayed payment because $121 million is numerically 
larger. 
D. If the appropriate discount rate were 12%, the present value of the delayed payment would not be less than $100 
million. 
E. The discount rate reﬂects the return available on a comparable alternative and the relevant risk of the cash ﬂow. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – $121 million / 1.10² = $100 million. 
B. True – $100 million × 1.10² = $121 million. 
C. False – The two payments are equivalent at a 10% opportunity cost under the stated assumptions. 
D. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Discounting by a higher rate produces $121 million / 1.12², 
which is about $96.46 million. 
E. True – Opportunity cost and risk are central to selecting an appropriate discount rate. 
 
[QF-D6G3Q2] 
An asset manager compares two risk-equivalent contracts. Contract A pays $50,000 at the end of year 1, and Contract B 
pays $50,000 at the end of year 4. The appropriate annual discount rate is positive. 
 
A. Contract A has the higher present value. 
B. The two contracts have equal present value because their nominal payments are equal. 
C. Increasing the discount rate widens the present-value disadvantage of the later payment. 
D. At a zero discount rate, the two payments would not have equal present value. 
E. A reviewer would be wrong to accept the claim that receiving cash earlier creates ﬂexibility to reinvest it before year 4. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – The same payment is discounted for fewer periods when received at the end of year 1. 
B. False – Timing affects present value whenever the discount rate is nonzero. 
C. True – The longer-dated payment is more sensitive to a change in the discount rate. 
D. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. With no discounting, both present values equal their $50,000 
nominal amounts. 
E. False – The underlying claim is correct and should be accepted. Earlier receipt allows the investor to earn a return 
during the intervening years.

A university endowment sets aside $100,000 today for a commitment due in ﬁve years. It expects the funds to earn 6% 
annually, compounded annually, and forecasts that the commitment will be $130,000. 
 
A. The expected future value of the set-aside is approximately $133,823. 
B. Under the forecast, the set-aside is expected to cover the $130,000 commitment. 
C. Simple interest of $6,000 per year is the correct calculation under annual compounding. 
D. If the realized return is lower than 6%, the ending value will be lower, all else equal. 
E. The present value of the $130,000 commitment at 6% is greater than $130,000. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – $100,000 × 1.06⁵ is approximately $133,823. 
B. True – The expected future value exceeds the forecast commitment by about $3,823. 
C. False – Annual compounding earns returns on earlier returns, so growth is not limited to simple interest. 
D. True – A lower compound growth rate produces a smaller future value. 
E. False – At a positive discount rate, its present value is less than its future nominal amount. 
 
G4: Discounted Cash Flow and Dividend Valuation 
[QF-D6G4Q1] 
An analyst values a stable dividend-paying company using a dividend discount model. The chief ﬁnancial officer argues 
that increasing the discount rate should increase the stock's estimated value because investors would be demanding a 
higher return. 
 
A. A higher discount rate generally lowers the present value of future dividends. 
B. A lower discount rate generally raises estimated intrinsic value. 
C. A reviewer would be wrong to accept the claim that the discount rate represents an opportunity cost or required return. 
D. Holding dividends constant, intrinsic value is unaffected by the discount rate. 
E. Small changes in discount-rate assumptions cannot materially affect valuation. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
 354 Rev:081020261230 
 
A. True – Future cash ﬂows are discounted more heavily. 
B. True – Lower discounting raises present value. 
C. False – The underlying claim is correct and should be accepted. The discount rate reﬂects the return required for 
bearing risk or foregoing alternatives. 
D. False – Discount rate is a key input in present-value calculations. 
E. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Long-duration cash ﬂows are especially sensitive to the 
discount rate. 
 
[QF-D6G4Q2] 
A utility is expected to pay a dividend of $3.00 one year from now, with dividends growing at 4% perpetually. An analyst 
uses a 9% required return and the constant-growth dividend discount model. The investment committee asks about the 
resulting valuation. 
 
A. The model estimates value as $3.00 / (0.09 - 0.04) = $60.00 per share. 
B. Raising the required return to 10%, while holding other assumptions ﬁxed, would reduce estimated value to $50.00. 
C. Raising perpetual growth to 5%, while holding the 9% required return ﬁxed, would reduce estimated value. 
D. The model requires the perpetual growth rate to be below the required return for a ﬁnite positive value. 
E. The $60.00 estimate is certain because the formula is mathematically precise. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – The Gordon growth formula gives $3.00 / 0.05 = $60.00. 
B. True – At a 6% spread between return and growth, value is $3.00 / 0.06 = $50.00. 
C. False – A higher growth rate narrows the denominator and raises the model value. 
D. True – If growth equals or exceeds the discount rate, the standard perpetuity formula does not yield a ﬁnite positive 
value. 
E. False – The output is highly dependent on uncertain dividend, growth, and required-return assumptions.

B. Projected year-3 free cash ﬂow is approximately $6.30 million. 
C. The three-year cumulative growth rate is exactly 24% because 8% × 3 = 24%. 
D. Compounding causes the year-3 forecast to depend on growth applied to earlier growth.

C. False – Compound growth is about 25.97%, not exactly 24%. 
D. True – Each year's growth builds on the prior year's enlarged cash ﬂow. 
E. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. The starting value scales the entire projected cash-ﬂow path.

G6: Margin of Safety and Scenario Analysis 
[QF-D6G6Q1] 
A research team estimates bear, base, and bull values of $40, $60, and $90 for a stock, with probabilities of 25%, 50%, 
and 25%, respectively. The shares trade at $50. The portfolio manager asks whether the scenario analysis supports a 
purchase. 
 
A. The probability-weighted estimated value is not $62.50. 
B. The market price is 20% below the probability-weighted estimate when the discount is measured relative to estimated 
value. 
C. A reviewer would be wrong to accept the claim that the bear case implies a 20% loss from the current market price. 
D. The probability-weighted value guarantees a positive realized return. 
E. The scenario framework makes the consequences of differing outcomes more visible than a single point estimate 
alone. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. 0.25($40) + 0.50($60) + 0.25($90) = $62.50. 
B. True – ($62.50 - $50) / $62.50 = 20%. 
C. False – The underlying claim is correct and should be accepted. A decline from $50 to $40 is a 20% loss. 
D. False – One outcome will occur, probabilities may be wrong, and price may not converge to modeled value. 
E. True – Multiple scenarios expose downside, central, and upside assumptions rather than hiding them in one number.

G7: Valuation-Based Long and Short Decisions 
[QF-D6G7Q1] 
A fund estimates that a stock worth $80 trades at $60. Its bear-case value is $45, and management may announce a 
strategic review within six months. The portfolio manager considers a long position. 
 
A. Convergence from $60 to the $80 estimate would not produce a 33.3% price gain. 
B. A decline from $60 to the $45 bear case would produce a 25% price loss. 
C. The possible strategic review could serve as a catalyst, but its occurrence and effect are uncertain. 
D. Buying below estimated value removes timing risk. 
E. Position sizing should reﬂect downside scenarios as well as the estimated upside. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. ($80 - $60) / $60 is approximately 33.3%. 
B. True – ($60 - $45) / $60 = 25%. 
C. True – A catalyst may prompt reassessment, but it is neither certain to occur nor certain to raise price. 
D. False – Price can remain below estimated value or fall further for an extended period. 
E. True – The payoff distribution, not only the central valuation estimate, should inform risk taken.
