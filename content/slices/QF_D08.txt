G1: Weak-Form Market Efficiency 
[QF-D8G1Q1] 
A researcher ﬁnds that a trading rule based only on the previous ﬁve days of prices and volume earned positive returns in 
the historical sample. She concludes that weak-form market efficiency has been disproved. 
 
 378 Rev:081020261230 
 
A. Weak-form efficiency concerns whether historical market data can be used to earn abnormal returns consistently. 
B. A proﬁtable in-sample result alone is sufficient to disprove weak-form efficiency. 
C. Data snooping could produce an apparently successful historical rule even when no durable predictability exists. 
D. Transaction costs and out-of-sample performance are relevant when evaluating the claimed inefficiency. 
E. Weak-form efficiency implies that a stock's price can never rise on two consecutive days. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – Weak-form efficiency states that information contained in past prices and volume is already reﬂected in current 
prices. 
B. False – The result may be sample-speciﬁc, overﬁt, or uneconomic after costs and therefore does not by itself establish 
exploitable inefficiency. 
C. True – Searching many rules can identify a chance winner in historical data. 
D. True – A usable abnormal return must survive independent testing and realistic implementation costs. 
E. False – Random or weakly predictable returns can still produce runs of gains or losses. 
 
[QF-D8G1Q2] 
A stock closes at $100. Under a simpliﬁed random-walk model, tomorrow's price equals today's price plus an 
unpredictable innovation with an expected value of zero. 
 
A. The model's expected price for tomorrow is not $100. 
B. The model guarantees that tomorrow's realized price will be $100. 
C. New information can cause tomorrow's realized price to differ substantially from $100. 
D. A random-walk intuition means price changes are independent of all possible information, including news released 
tomorrow. 
E. If price changes are unpredictable, a chart can still describe the past without necessarily forecasting abnormal future 
returns. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. With a zero-mean innovation, the conditional expected next 
price is the current price. 
B. False – The innovation is uncertain, so the realized price need not equal its expectation.

C. True – Unexpected information can be represented by a large positive or negative innovation. 
D. False – The model concerns predictability using currently available information; future news can affect future prices. 
E. True – Descriptive patterns do not automatically provide proﬁtable predictive power. 
 
[QF-D8G1Q3] 
An analyst tests a momentum signal derived exclusively from past returns. Before costs, the signal earns 6% per year 
above the benchmark; realistic turnover costs are estimated at 7% per year. 
 
A. The gross backtest suggests predictability based on historical price information. 
B. Under the stated cost estimate, the strategy earns approximately -1% per year relative to the benchmark after costs. 
C. A negative net result provides investors with an economically exploitable violation of weak-form efficiency. 
D. The ﬁnding could still be academically interesting even if the signal cannot be traded proﬁtably after costs. 
E. Lowering turnover while preserving enough of the signal could not change the strategy's net economics. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – The pre-cost result is associated with a signal constructed from past returns. 
B. True – The approximate net abnormal return is 6% - 7% = -1% per year. 
C. False – An opportunity that loses money after realistic costs is not economically exploitable as described. 
D. True – Predictability and practical proﬁtability are related but distinct research questions. 
E. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Reducing implementation costs could allow some of the gross 
edge to survive. 
 
G2: Semi-Strong Market Efficiency 
[QF-D8G2Q1] 
A company releases unexpectedly strong earnings at 8:30 a.m. The stock price jumps before the market opens. A junior 
analyst claims that the semi-strong form of market efficiency requires the stock to keep rising for several days because 
the news was public. 
 
A. Semi-strong efficiency implies public information should be incorporated rapidly into price. 
B. A delayed multi-day drift is required by semi-strong efficiency. 
C. Fundamental analysis based only on already-public information should consistently earn abnormal returns under 
semi-strong efficiency. 
 
 380 Rev:081020261230 
D. The immediate pre-market price jump is not consistent with semi-strong efficiency. 
E. Semi-strong efficiency does not claim that prices are always correct in an absolute sense. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – Semi-strong efficiency concerns rapid incorporation of public information. 
B. False – Persistent predictable drift would challenge the hypothesis. 
C. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Public information should already be reﬂected in prices. 
D. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Rapid adjustment is consistent with the hypothesis. 
E. True – Efficiency concerns information incorporation, not perfect valuation.

A company publishes an annual report at noon. At 2:00 p.m., an analyst reads the report, values the company using only 
the disclosed ﬁgures, and plans to buy before other investors notice the information. 
 
A. The annual report is public information once released. 
B. Semi-strong efficiency predicts that the market must wait until every investor reads the report before adjusting the 
price. 
C. Competition among analysts and algorithms can cause the price to respond before this particular analyst completes 
the valuation. 
D. If the analyst repeatedly earns abnormal risk-adjusted returns from newly released reports after costs, that evidence

E. Semi-strong efficiency rules out returns earned from bearing systematic risk. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – A published annual report is publicly available information. 
B. False – Prices may adjust through the trades of informed market participants without every investor processing the 
news. 
C. True – Other participants can analyze and trade on the disclosure rapidly. 
D. True – Consistent abnormal performance based on public disclosures would be evidence against the hypothesis. 
E. False – The hypothesis addresses abnormal returns from public information, not compensation for systematic risk.

0.4% over the next ten minutes, and then trades in a narrow range. 
 
A. The rapid initial movement is consistent with fast incorporation of public news. 
B. Semi-strong efficiency requires the ﬁrst transaction after the announcement to establish the exact ﬁnal price with no 
subsequent trading. 
C. A brief period of price discovery and disagreement can occur even in a relatively efficient market. 
D. The 0.4% rebound by itself proves that the announcement was ignored initially. 
E. Testing efficiency would not require a systematic analysis of whether predictable abnormal returns remain after the

Answer Key:

B. False – Efficiency does not require instantaneous perfection or eliminate price discovery.

not proof. 
E. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. The relevant issue is whether a reliable, tradeable pattern 
persists beyond chance and costs. 
 
G3: Strong-Form Market Efficiency 
[QF-D8G3Q1] 
A pharmaceutical executive privately learns that a major clinical trial failed before the result is announced. The executive 
believes strong-form market efficiency means the information must already be reﬂected in the company's stock price. 
 
A. Strong-form efficiency claims that prices reﬂect both public and private information. 
B. The executive's ability to proﬁt from genuinely private information would contradict the strong form. 
C. Semi-strong efficiency necessarily predicts that the failed trial is already reﬂected before public disclosure. 
D. Information can be economically valuable before it is legally permissible to trade on it. 
E. Insider-trading laws are proof that the strong form always holds in practice.

Answer Key: 
 
A. True – The strong form extends the information set to private as well as public information. 
B. True – Consistent abnormal proﬁts based on nonpublic information are inconsistent with the strong-form claim. 
C. False – Semi-strong efficiency concerns public information, so it does not require undisclosed results to be in the 
price. 
D. True – Trading restrictions do not eliminate the informational value of private news. 
E. False – Regulation restricts behavior; it does not demonstrate that prices already contain all private information.

A study ﬁnds that corporate insiders' legally reported purchases are followed, on average, by positive abnormal returns. 
The purchases become public only after the insiders trade and ﬁle the required reports. 
 
A. The ﬁnding may indicate that insiders possessed information not fully reﬂected in price when they traded. 
B. Such a ﬁnding is not easier to reconcile with semi-strong efficiency than with strong-form efficiency at the time of the 
insider trades. 
C. Once the ﬁling becomes public, it enters the information set considered by semi-strong efficiency. 
D. Strong-form efficiency predicts that insiders can systematically proﬁt because they are better trained investors. 
E. The result alone proves that every reported insider purchase is proﬁtable. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – Insiders may act on private knowledge or superior internal context before public disclosure. 
B. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Semi-strong efficiency does not require private information to 
be reﬂected, while strong-form efficiency does. 
C. True – A public ﬁling is public information once it is released. 
D. False – Strong-form efficiency denies consistent abnormal proﬁts even to investors with private information. 
E. False – An average relationship does not imply that every individual observation succeeds.

D. True – Acquisition and analysis costs can reduce incentives and slow complete incorporation. 
E. False – The strong form includes private information; the semi-strong form is limited to public information. 
 
G4: Arbitrage and Law-of-One-Price Reasoning 
[QF-D8G4Q1] 
The same freely transferable stock trades at $50.00 in Market A and $50.40 in Market B. A trader can buy and sell 
simultaneously, with total transaction and transfer costs of $0.10 per share and no quantity limits. 
 
A. Buying in Market A and selling in Market B produces a gross spread of $0.40 per share. 
B. The stated net proﬁt is not $0.30 per share. 
C. The strategy requires the stock's fundamental value to rise. 
D. Simultaneous execution helps reduce the risk that one price changes before both sides are not completed. 
E. Repeated arbitrage trading would tend to raise the price in Market A and lower the price in Market B. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – The gross price difference is $50.40 - $50.00 = $0.40 per share. 
B. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. After $0.10 of total costs, the net spread is $0.30 per share. 
 
 384 Rev:081020261230 
C. False – The proﬁt comes from the contemporaneous price discrepancy, not a directional forecast of fundamental 
value. 
D. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Locking in both legs limits execution-related price risk. 
E. True – Buying pressure in A and selling pressure in B push the two prices toward convergence. 
 
[QF-D8G4Q2] 
A convertible security and a replicating portfolio promise identical future cash ﬂows under all relevant states. The 
convertible costs $102, while the replicating portfolio costs $100. Both can be traded without costs, ﬁnancing 
constraints, or default risk. 
 
A. The law of one price suggests the two positions should not have the same price under the stated assumptions. 
B. A trader could buy the $100 replicating portfolio and short the $102 convertible. 
C. The initial gross cash inﬂow from that trade is $2. 
D. Because the future cash ﬂows offset in every state, the described discrepancy represents a theoretical arbitrage. 
E. If the cash ﬂows were only highly correlated rather than identical, the trade would remain riskless. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Identical state-contingent cash ﬂows should command the 
same price in a frictionless market. 
B. True – The strategy buys the cheaper equivalent and sells the more expensive one. 
C. True – Short-sale proceeds of $102 less the $100 purchase produce $2 initially. 
D. True – The offsetting future obligations leave the initial price difference as proﬁt under the assumptions. 
E. False – High correlation does not ensure identical payoffs, so residual risk would remain.

G5: Statistical Arbitrage and Mean Reversion 
[QF-D8G5Q1] 
Two historically related stocks have a ﬁtted spread with a long-run mean of $0 and standard deviation of $2. The current 
spread is +$5. A pair-trading model interprets positive spreads as Stock A being expensive relative to Stock B. 
 
A. The current spread has a z-score of +2.5 under the stated estimates. 
B. A mean-reversion trade would generally short the relatively expensive Stock A and buy Stock B. 
C. The trade requires both stocks to fall in absolute price to succeed. 
D. A large positive z-score guarantees that the spread will converge. 
E. A structural change in the companies could make the historical spread relationship unreliable. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – The z-score is ($5 - $0) / $2 = +2.5. 
B. True – The position is designed to proﬁt if A becomes cheaper relative to B and the spread declines. 
C. False – The relative spread can narrow with both stocks rising, both falling, or moving in opposite directions. 
D. False – A z-score measures historical unusualness, not certain future convergence. 
E. True – Mergers, business-model changes, leverage, or other shifts can break the prior relationship.

A pair trade buys $100,000 of Stock B and shorts $100,000 of Stock A. Over the holding period, A rises 8% and B rises 5%, 
excluding costs. 
 
A. The long position in B earns $5,000. 
B. The short position in A loses $8,000. 
C. The pair trade earns $3,000 before costs. 
D. Equal long and short dollar amounts prevent losses if the spread moves against the trade. 
E. The result shows that a dollar-neutral trade cannot lose money even when both securities rise. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – The long gain is $100,000 × 5% = $5,000. 
B. True – The short loses $100,000 × 8% = $8,000. 
C. False – Net P&L is $5,000 - $8,000 = -$3,000 before costs. 
D. False – Dollar neutrality limits net dollar exposure but does not eliminate relative-price risk. 
E. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. The short rose more than the long, producing a loss despite 
balanced initial dollars.

B. True – Selection bias makes the winning in-sample result unusually favorable.

G6: Event-Driven and Relative-Value Strategies

A buyer agrees to acquire a target company for $50 per share in cash. The target trades at $47 after the announcement 
because investors believe the deal might fail or be delayed. 
 
A. The announced-deal spread is not $3 per share. 
B. Buying at $47 and receiving $50 at closing would not produce a $3 gain per share before costs. 
C. The $3 spread is a guaranteed arbitrage proﬁt once the merger is announced. 
D. Regulatory rejection or ﬁnancing failure could cause the target price to fall and generate a loss. 
E. The annualized return depends partly on how long the deal takes to close. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. The difference between the $50 offer and $47 market price is 
$3. 
B. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Successful closing at the stated price produces $50 - $47 = $3 
before costs. 
C. False – Completion is uncertain, so merger arbitrage is generally risky event-driven investing. 
D. True – Deal failure can remove the support of the offer price and expose the trader to downside. 
E. True – Earning the same spread sooner produces a higher annualized return than earning it later.

G7: Limits to Arbitrage and Market Efficiency 
[QF-D8G7Q1] 
A fund manager believes an overpriced stock will eventually fall from $120 to its estimated value of $80. The manager 
shorts it, but the stock ﬁrst rises to $170, causing margin calls and forcing the fund to close the position. 
 
A. The manager can have a correct long-run valuation thesis and still lose money on the trade. 
B. Timing uncertainty can prevent an investor from holding a position until convergence. 
C. The interim rise represents noise-trader risk for the short seller. 
D. Because the estimated fundamental value is $80, the broker must allow the fund unlimited time to maintain the short. 
E. Forced liquidation cannot allow mispricing to persist even when informed investors recognize it. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – A trade can fail because of its path and ﬁnancing even if the terminal valuation view is eventually correct. 
B. True – Capital and margin constraints make the time to convergence economically important. 
C. True – Other traders can push price farther from estimated value before correction. 
D. False – Brokers impose collateral and margin requirements based on current exposure, not the manager's valuation 
estimate. 
E. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Constrained informed capital cannot necessarily eliminate an 
inefficiency immediately.
