## Introduction (p.46)
- **Efficiency** = information that can affect prices **travels quickly** throughout a market and prices
  adjust accordingly.
- Arbitrage opportunities exist when buyer and seller have differing views of true price — perhaps due to
  **differences in available information**. The more efficient a market, the more relevant information is
  **equally available** to all participants.
- **Disclosure requirements** in law/regulation aim to improve efficiency; **insider-trading prohibitions**
  make it hard for insiders to exploit their information advantage.
- **Most criminal cases involving hedge fund personnel have been related to insider trading.**

## What Makes Markets Efficient? (p.46)
- Indirect measure of efficiency: **speed with which a stock's price adjusts** to company-relevant information.
- Examples:
  1. A mining company reports test borings yielded **less gold per ton** than expected → investors infer
     higher future cost per ounce → lower profits.
  2. **Lower-than-expected earnings** have a **double effect**: lower price for a given **P/E**, and reduced
     prospects may **lower the P/E** itself. Close followers rush to sell before others realize → **investor
     self-interest** makes new information reflected in prices quickly.
- **Markets vary in efficiency**: very high volume + significant transparency (wide disclosure), e.g.
  **NYSE**, are at the high end; **illiquid niche markets** with poor information transmission (e.g., a
  frontier market in an emerging economy) are much less efficient.
- Efficiency is crucial to whether **active management** (e.g., hedge funds) is cost-effective. In highly
  efficient markets, competing managers cause arbitrage opportunities to be **"arbitraged away" almost
  instantaneously**.

## Three Versions of the EMH (p.47)
- **Eugene Fama** first postulated the EMH in the **mid-1960s**; elaborated by others into three
  **increasingly rigorous** versions:
  1. **Weak form**: future asset prices **cannot be predicted using historical price and volume data**
     (widely available in the press and on Google/Yahoo Finance).
  2. **Semi-strong form**: prices **adjust immediately to all publicly available information**, including
     company fundamentals such as financial disclosures.
  3. **Strong form**: prices **adjust immediately to all relevant information, including that available to
     insiders**.
- Which form best approximates reality determines **which strategies can succeed**:
  - **Weak form true** → **technical analysis** (only historical price/volume) **cannot succeed**, but
    investors developing other relevant information (e.g., independent **fundamental** intrinsic-value
    estimates) **can** have and exploit an information advantage.
  - **Semi-strong true** → **technical *and* fundamental analysis cannot work**; value investors are wasting
    their time — value-arbitrage opportunities have already been arbitraged away.
  - **Strong true** → **even those trading on inside information cannot succeed** ("risking jail to little gain").

## Debates over the EMH (pp.47–48)
- **Most academic studies have supported the validity of some version of the EMH.** Layman's summary:
  Malkiel's *A Random Walk Down Wall Street*.
- **Different versions likely apply in different markets.** The **strongest** versions apply in the
  largest, most transparent, most liquid markets, e.g., **U.S. large-cap stocks**. There, numerous studies
  show very few managers persistently outperform indexes — **even fewer than would be expected by mere chance**.
- A casual reader might conclude active management/hedge funds can't succeed under EMH; the authors say this
  seems reasonable **for most managers**, **but such reasoning may be overdone**:
  - **Active managers may be precisely the agents who make a market efficient** — they have incentive to find
    any arbitrage opportunity and, facing competitors, to act on it as fast as possible.
  - So **hedge funds may contribute to the existence of efficient markets rather than being made unnecessary
    by them**. High fees may offer dubious value to clients, yet provide a general service by incentivizing
    greater market efficiency.

## Exam traps (book-grounded)
- Forms are **nested / increasingly rigorous**: weak (past price & volume) → semi-strong (all public info)
  → strong (all info incl. insider).
- Weak form rules out **technical** analysis only; fundamental analysis **can** still work under weak form.
- Semi-strong rules out **both** technical and fundamental analysis (on public information).
- Only the strong form rules out profiting from **insider** information.
- Efficiency is **not uniform** — it varies by market (NYSE high; frontier/illiquid low); strongest forms
  apply to U.S. large caps.
- EMH does **not** imply hedge funds are pointless per the authors — active managers help **create** efficiency.
- Per the book, disclosure rules and insider-trading prohibitions are tools **aimed at improving** efficiency
  (making information equally available).
- Lower-than-expected earnings can hit price **twice** (lower E at given P/E, and a lower P/E).
