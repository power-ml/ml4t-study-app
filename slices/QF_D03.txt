G1: Correlation and Co-Movement 
[QF-D3G1Q1] 
A family office holds two strategies with similar expected returns and volatilities. Their historical return correlation is -
0.60, and the investment committee asks whether this means one strategy reliably gains whenever the other loses. 
 
A. The negative correlation indicates that the strategies have tended to move in opposite directions, but not in every 
period. 
B. A correlation of -0.60 implies that 60% of one strategy's losses were exactly offset by gains in the other. 
 
 303 Rev:081020261230 
C. If the correlation moved from -0.60 to +0.60 while other inputs stayed ﬁxed, the volatility of a long-only combination 
would generally increase. 
D. Correlation alone establishes that movements in one strategy cause movements in the other. 
E. Correlation is not bounded between -1 and +1. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – Correlation summarizes a tendency toward opposite co-movement, not an exceptionless period-by-period rule. 
B. False – Correlation is a standardized measure of linear co-movement, not an offset percentage. 
C. True – A more positive covariance term increases portfolio variance when weights and volatilities are positive. 
D. False – A statistical association does not by itself establish causation. 
E. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Standardized correlation cannot fall below -1 or exceed +1. 
 
[QF-D3G1Q2] 
A pension fund compares Asset A with two possible diversiﬁers. Asset B has a 0.05 historical correlation with A, while 
Asset C has a 0.70 correlation with A; the chief investment officer proposes calling B “independent” and C “the same 
trade. ” 
 
A. Asset B's correlation near zero indicates little historical linear co-movement with Asset A. 
B. A 0.05 correlation proves that A and B are statistically and economically independent. 
C. All else equal, B would ordinarily offer more diversiﬁcation relative to A than C would. 
D. A 0.70 correlation means A and C always have returns with the same sign. 
E. The correlations should be interpreted with the sample period and estimation uncertainty in mind. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – A value near zero indicates little linear association in the observed sample. 
B. False – Zero or near-zero linear correlation does not rule out nonlinear dependence or other relationships. 
C. True – Lower correlation generally reduces the covariance contribution to the combined portfolio's variance. 
D. False – A strong positive correlation allows periods in which returns have different signs. 
E. True – Correlation is an estimate that can vary with the data window and market regime.

A multi-asset fund holds equal weights in two assets, each with 20% annual volatility. A risk analyst reports that their 
estimated correlation rose from 0.10 to 0.80, although expected returns and individual volatilities were unchanged; the 
risk committee must decide whether to reduce exposure. 
 
A. The rise in correlation increases the covariance between the assets under the stated assumptions. 
B. The portfolio's expected return must fall because the correlation rose. 
C. A reviewer would be wrong to accept the claim that the portfolio's volatility increases when the correlation rises from 
0.10 to 0.80, all else equal. 
D. The assets' unchanged stand-alone volatilities are sufficient to conclude that portfolio risk is unchanged. 
E. The change matters because portfolio risk depends on how holdings move together, not only on their individual risk. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – Covariance equals correlation times the two standard deviations, which are unchanged here. 
B. False – Correlation affects the portfolio variance calculation, not the weighted expected return under these 
assumptions. 
C. False – The underlying claim is correct and should be accepted. For positive weights, the larger covariance term raises 
portfolio variance and volatility. 
D. False – Portfolio risk also depends on covariance among holdings. 
E. True – Co-movement determines how much individual ﬂuctuations offset or reinforce one another. 
 
G2: Covariance and Portfolio Risk 
[QF-D3G2Q1] 
An asset manager considers equal weights in Funds X and Y . X has 10% annual volatility, Y has 20%, and their correlation 
is 0.50; a junior analyst claims their covariance is 0.50 because covariance and correlation are interchangeable. 
 
A. Using returns in decimal form, the covariance is not 0.50 × 0.10 × 0.20 = 0.010. 
B. Correlation is unitless, whereas covariance depends on the units used for returns. 
C. The correlation can be recovered by dividing covariance by the product of the two standard deviations. 
D. If both volatilities doubled while correlation stayed at 0.50, covariance would only double. 
E. A positive covariance indicates that the funds have tended to move in the same direction relative to their means. 
 
 
 305 Rev:081020261230 
Answer Key: 
 
A. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. The covariance is correlation multiplied by both standard 
deviations. 
B. True – Standardization removes units from correlation, while covariance retains product units. 
C. True – This is the deﬁning relationship when both standard deviations are nonzero. 
D. False – Doubling both standard deviations multiplies their product, and therefore covariance, by four. 
E. True – Positive covariance reﬂects a tendency for deviations from each mean to share the same sign. 
 
[QF-D3G2Q2] 
A portfolio contains 60% in Asset A and 40% in Asset B. Their annual variances are 0.0225 and 0.0100, and their 
covariance is 0.0030; the portfolio manager asks the quant team to explain the portfolio-variance calculation before 
approving the allocation. 
 
A. The two-asset variance is (0.60² × 0.0225) + (0.40² × 0.0100) + (2 × 0.60 × 0.40 × 0.0030). 
B. Under the stated inputs, the portfolio variance equals 0.01114. 
C. Omitting the covariance term would correctly measure portfolio variance because each asset's variance is already 
included. 
D. If covariance were lower while all other inputs stayed ﬁxed, portfolio variance would not be lower. 
E. Portfolio volatility is not the square root of portfolio variance, not the weighted average of the two variances. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – The expression includes both weighted variance terms and the cross-covariance term. 
B. True – The terms are 0.00810, 0.00160, and 0.00144, which sum to 0.01114. 
C. False – Omitting covariance ignores whether the assets' returns reinforce or offset each other. 
D. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. With positive weights, a lower covariance reduces the cross 
term directly. 
E. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Variances combine through the covariance formula, after which 
the square root gives volatility.

[QF-D3G2Q3] 
A risk committee compares two 50/50 portfolios. Both use assets with 15% annual volatility, but Portfolio P's pair has 
covariance 0.0180 while Portfolio Q's pair has covariance -0.0045; expected returns are identical, and the committee 
wants the lower-risk allocation. 
 
A. Portfolio P has the larger covariance contribution to portfolio variance. 
B. Portfolio Q can have lower volatility even though its component assets have the same stand-alone volatility as those 
in P . 
C. Negative covariance means that each asset in Q has negative stand-alone variance. 
D. Evaluating only the assets' individual volatilities would miss the principal difference between P and Q. 
E. Because expected returns are identical, covariance cannot affect the allocation decision. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – Its positive covariance produces a larger cross term than Q's negative covariance. 
B. True – Offsetting co-movement can lower combined risk without changing the component volatilities. 
C. False – Individual variances cannot be negative; the negative value describes joint movement. 
D. True – The portfolios differ in co-movement, which is essential to total portfolio variance. 
E. False – With equal expected return, the lower-risk portfolio may be preferable precisely because covariance differs. 
 
G3: Speciﬁc vs. Systematic Risk 
[QF-D3G3Q1] 
A long-only equity fund holds nearly all its capital in one pharmaceutical company awaiting a regulatory decision. The 
portfolio manager argues that the broad market is calm, so the fund's risk is low; the chief risk officer asks the team to 
separate company-speciﬁc from market-wide exposure. 
 
A. An adverse decision affecting only the company is not primarily a speciﬁc-risk event. 
B. Spreading the position across many unrelated companies could not reduce the portfolio's exposure to that single 
decision. 
C. A broad recession that depresses most equities is systematic risk from the fund's perspective. 
D. Holding more companies guarantees elimination of all equity-market risk. 
E. Low recent market volatility does not remove the concentrated company's event risk. 
 
 
 307 Rev:081020261230 
Answer Key: 
 
A. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. The regulatory outcome is tied to one issuer rather than the 
market as a whole. 
B. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Diversiﬁcation can reduce the impact of idiosyncratic 
outcomes from one company. 
C. True – Economy-wide shocks tend to affect many securities and cannot be diversiﬁed away within equities. 
D. False – Broad market exposure remains even in a well-diversiﬁed equity portfolio. 
E. True – Company-speciﬁc catalysts can produce large moves independently of aggregate market conditions. 
 
[QF-D3G3Q2] 
An index manager expands a portfolio from 20 to 500 stocks while maintaining roughly the same market and sector 
exposures. A client expects the expansion to eliminate both stock-selection risk and losses during market crashes. 
 
A. Increasing the number of reasonably diversiﬁed holdings can reduce ﬁrm-speciﬁc risk. 
B. The expansion necessarily reduces the portfolio's systematic market exposure to zero. 
C. A market-wide interest-rate shock can still affect many or all 500 holdings. 
D. The incremental reduction in speciﬁc risk is likely to diminish as the portfolio becomes broadly diversiﬁed. 
E. A 500-stock portfolio cannot experience a negative return caused by an economy-wide shock. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – Idiosyncratic gains and losses have more opportunity to offset across many issuers. 
B. False – Retaining similar market exposure preserves systematic risk. 
C. True – Common macroeconomic shocks can affect a broad cross-section of securities. 
D. True – Early additions remove more concentration, while later additions often provide smaller marginal beneﬁts. 
E. False – Diversiﬁcation does not immunize a portfolio against common market declines.

G4: Diversiﬁcation Beneﬁts 
[QF-D3G4Q1] 
A balanced fund considers combining two assets, each expected to return 8% annually with 12% volatility. Their 
correlation is zero, and the investment committee asks whether combining two risky assets can genuinely reduce risk 
without lowering expected return. 
 
A. A 50/50 portfolio retains an expected return of 8% under the stated assumptions. 
B. The 50/50 portfolio's variance is 0.5²(0.12²) + 0.5²(0.12²) because the covariance term is zero. 
C. The resulting portfolio volatility is not approximately 8.49%, below either asset's 12% volatility. 
D. Zero correlation means neither asset can ever lose in the same period as the other. 
E. This example demonstrates that risk reduction need not require a proportional reduction in expected return. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – Expected portfolio return is the weighted average, here 0.5(8%) + 0.5(8%). 
B. True – Zero correlation implies zero covariance when the volatilities are ﬁnite. 
C. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. The variance is 0.0072, whose square root is about 0.0849.

D. False – Zero linear correlation does not prohibit simultaneous losses. 
E. True – Expected return remains 8% while volatility falls through imperfect co-movement.

The strategy has a positive expected return but lower stand-alone return than the growth asset; trustees worry that any 
allocation to it must make the portfolio strictly worse. 
 
A. The defensive strategy's negative correlation can reduce total portfolio volatility. 
B. A lower stand-alone expected return automatically makes the defensive strategy undesirable in every portfolio. 
C. The portfolio may achieve a more attractive risk-return tradeoff even if its expected return falls modestly. 
D. Negative historical correlation guarantees protection in every future drawdown. 
E. The allocation decision should not consider joint portfolio outcomes rather than ranking assets solely by stand-alone 
return. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – Opposing co-movement can create a negative covariance contribution to portfolio variance. 
B. False – Portfolio value depends on both expected return and interaction with existing holdings. 
C. True – A sufficient reduction in risk may compensate for a smaller reduction in expected return. 
D. False – Correlations are estimates and can change across regimes. 
E. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Diversiﬁcation is inherently a portfolio-level concept.

G5: Concentration and Position Sizing 
[QF-D3G5Q1] 
A hedge fund manager wants to place 35% of fund capital in a single high-conviction stock. The investment thesis is 
strong, but the fund's mandate limits severe drawdowns; the risk committee is evaluating whether conviction justiﬁes 
the proposed size. 
 
A. If the stock loses 50% while all other holdings are unchanged, it reduces fund value by approximately 17.5% before 
secondary effects. 
B. A strong expected-return estimate eliminates the need to consider position size. 
C. The concentrated weight increases the portfolio's sensitivity to estimation error in the stock thesis. 
D. A position limit cannot constrain the damage from an issuer-speciﬁc event. 
E. Diversifying some capital away from the stock necessarily means the manager has no conviction in the thesis. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – A 35% weight multiplied by a 50% loss produces a 17.5% portfolio loss. 
B. False – Expected returns are uncertain, and sizing determines the consequence of being wrong. 
C. True – Errors in forecasts or omitted risks have a larger portfolio effect at a larger weight. 
D. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Limits cap direct exposure to a single issuer or thesis. 
E. False – Position sizing balances conviction with uncertainty and portfolio-level risk.

B. The eight bank positions may behave like a much smaller number of independent bets.

Answer Key:

E. True – Multiple limit dimensions address risks that security-level limits miss.

G6: Diminishing Marginal Diversiﬁcation 
[QF-D3G6Q1] 
A wealth manager begins with a single-stock portfolio and successively adds equal-sized positions from unrelated 
industries. A client sees risk fall sharply at ﬁrst but only slightly after dozens of additions and asks whether the process 
has stopped working. 
 
A. The ﬁrst few independent additions often remove more ﬁrm-speciﬁc concentration than later additions. 
B. A diminishing marginal beneﬁt means the later holdings increase risk in every case. 
C. As speciﬁc risk is diversiﬁed away, systematic risk becomes a larger share of the remaining portfolio risk. 
D. Adding inﬁnitely many stocks from the same market guarantees portfolio volatility will converge to zero. 
E. The observed pattern is consistent with diversiﬁcation beneﬁts approaching a ﬂoor set by common risks. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – Moving away from one or a few names initially removes substantial idiosyncratic exposure. 
B. False – Later additions may still reduce risk, just by progressively smaller amounts. 
C. True – Common market exposure remains after much of the issuer-speciﬁc risk has been diluted. 
D. False – Market-wide systematic risk cannot be eliminated merely by adding more stocks. 
E. True – Diminishing beneﬁts arise as the diversiﬁable component shrinks.

G7: Correlation Instability and Crisis Behavior 
[QF-D3G7Q1] 
A risk model estimates correlations using ﬁve calm years and predicts a maximum portfolio loss of 8%. During a liquidity 
crisis, several risky assets begin falling together; the chief risk officer asks why realized diversiﬁcation is weaker than the 
model projected. 
 
A. Correlations cannot rise during stress as assets respond to common deleveraging and liquidity shocks. 
B. Correlations estimated in calm periods are guaranteed to remain valid during crises. 
C. Higher positive correlations reduce the amount of offsetting movement among long positions. 
D. The model cannot understate crisis risk if it assumes a stable covariance matrix. 
E. Stress tests using adverse correlation scenarios can complement estimates based only on historical averages. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Shared funding pressure and simultaneous selling can 
synchronize returns. 
B. False – Dependence structures can change materially across market regimes. 
C. True – More synchronized losses weaken the protection expected from diversiﬁcation. 
D. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. A calm-period matrix may not represent stressed co-
movement. 
E. True – Scenario analysis tests the portfolio against plausible relationships absent from average history.
