G1: Investment Hypothesis and Signal Design 
[QF-D10G1Q1] 
A quant team ﬁnds that stocks with unusually high social-media mentions outperform over the next day. The portfolio 
manager asks whether the pattern justiﬁes a strategy, but requires an economic explanation and evidence that the data 
would have been available before trading. 
 
A. The observed correlation alone proves that social-media activity causes the subsequent returns. 
B. A defensible hypothesis should explain why mentions might predict returns and when the relationship should weaken 
or reverse. 
C. Testing whether the effect survives controls for news, liquidity, and recent returns can help distinguish the proposed 
mechanism from alternatives. 
D. The team should deﬁne the signal and trading rule before using an untouched sample for validation. 
E. A statistically signiﬁcant relationship necessarily remains proﬁtable after transaction costs. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. False – Correlation can arise from confounding, reverse causality, data leakage, or chance. 
B. True – A useful investment hypothesis identiﬁes a plausible mechanism and conditions governing the effect. 
C. True – Relevant controls test whether another known inﬂuence better explains the relationship. 
D. True – Freezing the design before validation reduces selection and tuning on the evaluation data. 
E. False – Statistical evidence does not establish sufficient economic magnitude or net proﬁtability.

An asset manager believes that slow-moving institutional demand creates medium-term price continuation. The 
research director asks the team to translate the idea into a measurable signal without merely choosing whichever 
lookback performed best historically. 
 
A. A past-return measure over a stated lookback could not operationalize the direction of the proposed continuation 
effect. 
B. The hypothesis predicts that stronger recent performance should always create a more negative position. 
C. Trading volume or fund-ﬂow measures could be tested as evidence related to the proposed demand mechanism.

D. Selecting the lookback solely because it maximizes full-sample performance increases overﬁtting risk. 
E. Pre-specifying expected behavior in liquid versus illiquid securities cannot make the hypothesis more falsiﬁable. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. A deﬁned past-return feature is one measurable representation 
of price continuation. 
B. False – Continuation implies that stronger recent performance generally maps toward a more positive, not more 
negative, forecast. 
C. True – These variables may provide evidence about whether persistent demand is actually present. 
D. True – Choosing the best result after searching the whole history capitalizes on sample-speciﬁc noise. 
E. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Conditional predictions create evidence that can support or 
contradict the proposed mechanism. 
 
[QF-D10G1Q3] 
A researcher reports that ﬁrms with ticker symbols beginning with letters A–M outperformed N–Z ﬁrms in one historical 
sample. The chief investment officer will fund further work only if the team can explain the effect and demonstrate that it 
is not a chance partition. 
 
A. The alphabetical pattern is automatically an investment hypothesis because it separates ﬁrms into two groups. 
B. Searching many arbitrary partitions can produce an apparently strong result by chance. 
C. Stability across different periods would be more persuasive than performance in the discovery sample alone. 
D. A plausible channel linking the feature to expected returns would strengthen the case for continued research. 
E. If the pattern has no economic rationale, increasing leverage is an appropriate substitute for validation. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. False – A measurable correlation is not by itself a defensible explanation of expected returns. 
B. True – Multiple testing creates many opportunities to discover spurious relationships. 
C. True – Temporal replication helps assess whether the ﬁnding generalizes beyond its selected sample. 
D. True – A mechanism supports conditional predictions and helps distinguish signal from coincidence. 
E. False – Leverage magniﬁes exposure; it does not create evidence that the signal is genuine.

G2: Forecasts, Positions, and Trades 
[QF-D10G2Q1] 
A model predicts next-day returns of +0.04%, +0.01%, and -0.03% for three stocks. The trader estimates a 0.02% round-
trip cost for each and asks whether every nonzero forecast should become a trade. 
 
A. The sign of a forecast can help determine position direction, but it does not alone determine whether to trade. 
B. Before considering risk, the +0.01% forecast is not smaller than its estimated round-trip cost. 
C. A no-trade threshold cannot prevent forecasts too small to cover expected costs from generating positions. 
D. Forecast magnitude and uncertainty can both be inputs to position sizing. 
E. Because the ﬁrst forecast is positive, a long position is guaranteed to earn a proﬁt. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – Action also depends on magnitude, conﬁdence, costs, risk, and portfolio constraints. 
B. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. The expected gross edge is 0.01%, compared with a 0.02% cost 
estimate. 
C. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Thresholding can reserve trades for signals with sufficient 
expected net value. 
D. True – Larger and more reliable forecasts may justify different exposure than weak or uncertain ones. 
E. False – A forecast is uncertain and realized returns can differ from its predicted value. 
 
[QF-D10G2Q2] 
A classiﬁer correctly predicts whether the market will close up or down on 60% of days. The portfolio manager notes that 
its correct calls tend to occur on tiny moves while several incorrect calls coincide with large market declines. 
 
A. Directional accuracy alone is sufficient to establish trading proﬁtability. 
B. The magnitude of returns on correct and incorrect days affects strategy P&L. 
C. A 60% hit rate can coexist with a negative average trading return. 
D. Position sizing that ignores forecast conﬁdence and downside exposure cannot worsen the large-error problem. 
E. Evaluating a return-weighted objective can provide information that classiﬁcation accuracy omits. 
 
Answer Key:

A. False – Accuracy treats small and large outcomes alike and ignores costs and sizing. 
B. True – Trading P&L depends on the signed size of each realized outcome, not only the number correct. 
C. True – A few large losses can outweigh many small gains. 
D. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Equal or aggressive sizing can assign too much capital to 
uncertain, high-consequence predictions. 
E. True – Return-based evaluation reﬂects economic magnitudes more directly than hit rate. 
 
[QF-D10G2Q3] 
A market-neutral fund converts stock-return forecasts into positions proportional to predicted return. The risk manager 
observes that the model repeatedly assigns its largest positions to volatile, thinly traded stocks and requires a safer 
construction rule. 
 
A. Raw forecast-proportional sizing can concentrate risk in securities with large forecast magnitudes. 
B. Scaling forecasts by volatility cannot help make position risk more comparable across securities. 
C. Liquidity and position limits can constrain trades even when a forecast is strong. 
D. Dollar neutrality guarantees neutrality to every market, sector, and factor exposure. 
E. Converting forecasts into portfolio weights is not a separate design problem from producing the forecasts. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – Large predictions mechanically receive large weights unless additional controls intervene. 
B. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Volatility scaling reduces the allocation to a high-risk security 
for a given forecast. 
C. True – Tradability and concentration constraints can override an unconstrained signal mapping. 
D. False – Equal long and short dollars can retain beta, sector, style, and other systematic exposures. 
E. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Portfolio construction must translate predictive information 
into feasible, risk-controlled actions.

committee asks the researcher to compare the strategy with meaningful alternatives before claiming success.

A. A zero-return cash benchmark alone is sufficient to determine whether the equity selection model added value. 
B. The investable index is relevant because it represents a passive exposure available to the strategy. 
C. Under the stated returns, the strategy underperformed the index by 2 percentage points annually before further risk 
adjustment. 
D. Differences in beta, sector exposure, risk, costs, and constraints cannot matter when attributing the performance gap. 
E. Comparing with a simple signal or naive allocation can reveal whether model complexity adds incremental value.

[QF-D10G3Q3] 
A sector-rotation strategy beats the broad market, but the performance report shows that it maintained a persistent 
overweight to technology during a technology-led rally. The client asks how much return came from the timing model 
rather than static exposure. 
 
A. Broad-market outperformance alone proves that the rotation decisions added alpha. 
B. A sector-matched or exposure-matched benchmark can improve performance attribution. 
C. Comparing the strategy with a ﬁxed portfolio holding its average sector weights can help isolate timing value. 
D. Factor regression may reveal that known systematic exposures explain part of the excess return. 
E. Attribution is unnecessary if the backtest's total return is positive. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. False – A persistent sector tilt can explain the result without successful rotation. 
B. True – Matching relevant exposures creates a more informative counterfactual. 
C. True – The comparison separates dynamic changes from the return of the average allocation. 
D. True – Estimated factor loadings can identify compensation for systematic risks rather than unique skill. 
E. False – Investors need to know what exposures produced returns and whether they match the intended process. 
 
G4: Turnover, Costs, and Net Performance

earns 8% annually before costs. The head of trading estimates that spreads, commissions, and market impact would 
total roughly 10% annually at the proposed turnover. 
 
A. The strategy is not proﬁtable on a gross basis. 
B. The strategy is proﬁtable on a net basis under the cost estimate. 
C. High turnover cannot cause a statistically useful signal to become economically unviable. 
D. Transaction costs should be incorporated before deciding whether to deploy the strategy. 
 
 414 Rev:081020261230 
E. Reducing turnover could improve net performance even if gross predictive accuracy falls slightly. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Gross return is positive before costs. 
B. False – Estimated costs exceed gross return. 
C. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Small edges can be consumed by implementation costs. 
D. True – Deployment decisions should be based on net, not gross, performance. 
E. True – Lower trading intensity may improve net economics. 
 
[QF-D10G4Q2] 
A daily strategy manages $10 million and turns over 40% of portfolio value each day, where turnover denotes the dollar 
value traded divided by portfolio value. The execution team estimates an average one-way trading cost of 0.10% of dollars 
traded and asks for a ﬁrst-order cost estimate. 
 
A. The strategy trades approximately $4 million per day under the stated turnover deﬁnition. 
B. The estimated daily trading cost is approximately $4,000. 
C. If gross expected proﬁt is $3,000 per day, the strategy remains proﬁtable after the estimated trading cost. 
D. Reducing unnecessary rebalancing can improve net returns without changing the underlying forecast model. 
E. The 0.10% estimate must remain constant regardless of order size, liquidity, or market conditions. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – 40% × $10 million = $4 million traded. 
B. True – 0.10% × $4 million = $4,000. 
C. False – $3,000 - $4,000 = a $1,000 estimated daily net loss. 
D. True – A turnover-aware position rule can reduce implementation drag separately from prediction. 
E. False – Spreads and market impact can vary with size, liquidity, volatility, and execution conditions.

A. False – Scaling can worsen execution and prevent the fund from obtaining the historical positions at observed prices. 
B. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Larger demand often walks the order book or moves other 
participants' quotes.

C. True – Such limits restrict trading to a manageable portion of market liquidity. 
D. True – Slower execution trades impact against the risk that prices move before completion.

G6: Leverage, Exposure, and Risk Controls 
[QF-D10G6Q1] 
A fund has $10 million of equity and uses borrowed funds to hold $30 million of a risky portfolio, creating 3-to-1 gross 
exposure relative to equity. The risk committee asks how leverage changes outcomes before ﬁnancing costs. 
 
A. A 2% gain on the risky portfolio produces approximately a 6% gain on fund equity before costs. 
B. A reviewer would be wrong to accept the claim that a 2% loss on the risky portfolio produces approximately a 6% loss 
on fund equity before costs. 
C. Leverage magniﬁes gains but leaves losses unchanged. 
D. Adverse moves can trigger margin calls or forced deleveraging even if the long-run thesis remains valid. 
E. Financing cost and availability cannot affect the realized beneﬁt of leverage. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. True – 2% × $30 million = $0.6 million, or 6% of $10 million equity. 
B. False – The underlying claim is correct and should be accepted. A $0.6 million loss is likewise 6% of fund equity. 
C. False – Leverage magniﬁes both favorable and adverse portfolio returns. 
D. True – Lenders and brokers impose collateral requirements based on current conditions. 
E. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Borrowing is neither free nor guaranteed to remain available.

G7: Robustness, Degradation, and Live Deployment 
[QF-D10G7Q1] 
A model performed well in a low-volatility training period but loses money after deployment during a high-volatility regime. 
The research lead asks whether the model should be retrained immediately or whether the failure reﬂects a deeper 
change. 
 
A. A regime shift cannot change feature distributions and the relationship between features and returns. 
B. Retraining on the most recent observations always restores proﬁtable performance. 
C. Comparing live inputs and outcomes with training distributions can help diagnose drift. 
D. Performance by volatility regime can reveal dependence hidden by an overall backtest average. 
E. The team should consider recalibration, redesign, or retirement if the original economic relationship no longer holds. 
 
Answer Key: 
 
A. False – The statement reverses the correct conclusion. Both the inputs and the conditional return process can change 
across regimes. 
B. False – Recent data may be limited or noisy, and the predictive relationship may have disappeared. 
C. True – Distribution and residual monitoring can identify ways deployment differs from training. 
D. True – Aggregate results can conceal that success was concentrated in one environment. 
E. True – Retraining is not the only response when the underlying mechanism has degraded.
